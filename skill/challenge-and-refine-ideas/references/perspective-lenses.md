# Perspective lenses and adviser routing

Use this reference to choose independent viewpoints that expose different assumptions, stakeholders, evidence, or failure modes. Select the smallest useful set; a lens is a question discipline, not a job title.

## Contents

1. Selection rules
2. Core lenses
3. Useful combinations
4. Adviser independence and external grounding
5. Adviser packet and memo quality

## 1. Selection rules

Choose a lens only when it can change a live decision. Prefer diversity across these dimensions:

- stakeholder or beneficiary;
- time horizon;
- value criterion;
- evidence source;
- construction versus criticism;
- normal use versus failure or abuse;
- current system versus alternative frame.

Avoid selecting three lenses that all reward growth, elegance, safety, or feasibility in the same way. Do not use more than one lens merely to confirm the preferred answer.

Before activation, write one sentence for how each selected lens differs in stakeholder, criterion, time horizon, evidence source, or causal model. If two lenses cannot be distinguished this way, merge or replace one.

## 2. Core lenses

### Purpose and strategy

Ask whether the idea advances the desired future, why now matters, what it intentionally refuses to optimize, and whether a different problem deserves attention first. Look for activity mistaken for progress.

### User or beneficiary

Start from the person's situation, struggle, current alternative, trust, effort, incentives, adoption path, and reason to stop using the idea. Look for a solution searching for a user.

### Experience and narrative

Walk through discovery, first use, normal use, failure, recovery, and long-term relationship. Make abstract principles concrete. Look for moments where the idea feels confusing, coercive, embarrassing, or inconsistent.

### Simplicity and focus

Find the smallest version that preserves the central value. Identify features, audiences, promises, or processes that dilute the idea. Ask what can be deleted and what must remain unmistakable.

### Alternative frame

Challenge the assumed category, mechanism, business model, medium, audience, or ownership model. Offer at least one materially different way to achieve the purpose, including improving the current alternative or doing nothing.

### Creative provocation

Introduce a useful inversion, analogy, extreme constraint, unfamiliar medium, or counterintuitive principle. Generate difference, not random novelty. Reconnect every provocation to the user's purpose.

### Feasibility and resources

Test dependencies, capabilities, time, cost, information, coordination, and implementation uncertainty. Distinguish impossible, difficult, expensive, and merely unfamiliar. Suggest a smaller proof when full feasibility is unknown.

### Economics and incentives

Ask who pays, who benefits, who bears cost or risk, what behavior is rewarded, what becomes cheaper or more expensive at scale, and whether incentives undermine the stated purpose. Do not invent financial precision without data.

### Operations and lifecycle

Consider ownership after launch, support, exceptions, maintenance, change, misuse, failure recovery, and retirement. Look for ideas that work only at the moment of presentation.

### Evidence and experiment

Separate preferences from testable claims. Identify the assumption with the highest combination of uncertainty and consequence. Design the smallest observation or experiment that can discriminate between options.

### Risk, ethics, and abuse

Consider safety, privacy, fairness, manipulation, exclusion, power asymmetry, legal or reputational exposure, and adversarial use. Connect each concern to a plausible scenario and proportionate safeguard; avoid generic fear lists.

### Skeptic and red team

Steelman the idea, then find the strongest reason it may fail, harm trust, solve the wrong problem, or be dominated by an alternative. State what evidence would defeat the objection. Do not oppose for performance.

### Second-order and long-term

Ask what happens after adoption, imitation, scaling, dependency, regulation, changed incentives, or user adaptation. Look for success creating the next problem and temporary choices becoming permanent obligations.

### Delivery and decision

Translate the current idea into a bounded next commitment with owner, sequence, dependencies, decision criteria, and stop conditions. Use only after purpose and value are coherent.

## 3. Useful combinations

Use combinations as starting points, then adapt them to the actual uncertainty.

| Idea type | Constructive lenses | Required counter-lens |
|---|---|---|
| New product or feature | user, simplicity, feasibility | skeptic or evidence |
| Business or service | user, economics, operations | alternative frame or risk |
| Creative concept | experience, creative provocation, simplicity | audience skeptic |
| Strategy or policy | purpose, incentives, second-order | affected-stakeholder or red team |
| Project or initiative | purpose, feasibility, delivery | no-action alternative |
| High-impact decision | evidence, risk, second-order | independent challenger |
| Existing idea needing improvement | experience, simplicity, operations | counterexample lens |

For a deeply personal choice, use values, alternatives, reversibility, and future-self perspectives without pretending to be a therapist. Treat an `affected-stakeholder`, `audience`, `user`, or `future-self` Agent as a modeled hypothesis, never as testimony from a real person. For medical, legal, or financial decisions, use qualified evidence and professional review rather than an Agent council as the authority.

## 4. Adviser independence and external grounding

Label adviser independence honestly:

- **L0 — serial lens:** one facilitator applies multiple lenses in the same context. Fast, but correlated.
- **L1 — isolated memo:** a separate adviser receives packet-only context, one question, and no inherited conversation or other opinions.
- **L2 — blind challenge:** an independent challenger evaluates a neutral current candidate and decision criteria without facilitator advocacy or other conclusions.

Report external grounding separately as `none`, `primary-source`, `field`, `experimental`, or `operational`. A serial facilitator may be L0 while still using primary-source evidence; an isolated Agent may be L1 while using no external grounding.

Different Agent names do not automatically create L1/L2 independence. Shared prompts, models, data, and assumptions can create correlated error. If the runtime cannot prevent inherited context, report L0 regardless of physical Agent count.

## 5. Adviser packet and memo quality

Give an adviser one perspective, one central question, and one bounded output. Include the user's wording, relevant facts, constraints, and decision criteria. Exclude the facilitator's desired conclusion, other memos, unrelated conversation history, and unnecessary sensitive details.

A useful memo:

- makes one or a few decision-relevant claims;
- shows its assumptions and confidence;
- includes a concrete example, counterexample, or scenario;
- proposes an improvement, alternative, or discriminating test;
- identifies what evidence would change the view;
- ends when the bounded question is answered.

Use the common memo core from `SKILL.md`, then request only lens-specific fields that matter. Examples:

| Lens | Useful lens-specific field |
|---|---|
| User/beneficiary | adoption barrier or trust-breaking moment |
| Experience | scenario breakpoint and recovery |
| Simplicity | minimum essence and deletion candidate |
| Alternative frame | genuinely different mechanism or no-action path |
| Feasibility | blocking dependency and smallest proof |
| Economics | incentive conflict and who bears downside |
| Operations | ownership/failure obligation after launch |
| Evidence | decision-changing claim and discriminating observation |
| Risk/abuse | plausible harm path and proportionate safeguard |
| Creative provocation | useful inversion and where it breaks |
| Red team | steelman, strongest rejection, failure path, repair, falsifier |

After receiving memos, compare their premises and implications. If they converge for the same reason, call that one correlated argument—not multiple confirmations. Add a missing opposing criterion or external evidence only when it could change the decision.

Reject a memo that only summarizes the idea, lists generic pros and cons, performs a role stereotype, asserts expertise without evidence, or recommends more research without saying what to learn.
