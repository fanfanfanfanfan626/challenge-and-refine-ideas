# Adaptive discussion methods

Use these methods selectively to reduce the most important uncertainty. Do not run them as a checklist.

## Contents

1. Framing methods
2. Expansion methods
3. Challenge methods
4. Convergence methods
5. Question and round design

## 1. Framing methods

### Open narrative

Invite the user to describe the frustration, desired future, example, or intuition in natural language. Listen for repeated phrases, emotional weight, implicit boundaries, and what they do not want to lose.

### Laddering and Five Whys

Move from a proposed feature or tactic toward the underlying outcome, belief, or value. Stop when another “why” would become speculative or irritating. Reflect the chain and let the user correct it.

### Jobs and situation framing

Describe who is trying to make what progress, in which situation, against which current alternative, with what anxieties or constraints. Use this beyond products whenever a person or organization is trying to change state.

### Example and counterexample

Ask for something that clearly belongs and something that would feel wrong even if it appeared successful. Extract the principle that separates them.

## 2. Expansion methods

### Scenario walkthrough

Walk through before, discovery, first attempt, normal use, exception, failure, recovery, and long-term relationship. Add only details that change experience, feasibility, responsibility, or trust.

### Option branching

Create two or three materially different approaches based on different principles, not cosmetic variants. Include no action, a manual solution, or an improvement to the status quo when credible.

### Analogy transfer

Borrow a mechanism from another domain, then explicitly test where the analogy breaks. Keep the useful structural insight; discard superficial resemblance.

### Constraint inversion

Temporarily make a constraint extreme—one day, one user, no automation, no budget, perfect privacy, or tenfold scale—to reveal what is essential. Return to realistic conditions before recommending.

### Desired-future backcast

Describe the observable future in which the idea worked, then reason backward to the earliest necessary change, evidence, and decision. Avoid writing a victory story with no causal path.

## 3. Challenge methods

### Assumption-confidence map

List only assumptions whose failure would materially change the idea. Rate confidence qualitatively and identify the cheapest evidence for the most dangerous unknown.

### Contradiction matrix

Surface tensions such as simplicity versus control, privacy versus personalization, speed versus reliability, reach versus exclusivity, or growth versus trust. Determine whether the conflict can be designed around or requires a human trade-off.

### Pre-mortem

Assume the idea failed or harmed trust. Describe the plausible path, earliest signal, containment, and which current assumption allowed it. Avoid implausible catastrophe lists.

### Inversion and rejection test

Ask what would have to be true for rejecting, delaying, simplifying, or reversing the idea to be the better decision. This exposes hidden commitment and sunk-cost reasoning.

### Steelman then red-team

State the strongest coherent version before attacking it. Challenge the strongest causal link, not the weakest wording. Say what evidence would defeat the objection.

### Stakeholder swap

Re-evaluate the idea from the perspective of the person who pays, uses, maintains, is excluded, bears downside, handles failure, or must trust it. Use concrete effects, not role-play theater.

## 4. Convergence methods

### Decision criteria

Name the few criteria that actually decide between options. Separate threshold requirements from preferences. Let the human set value weights rather than hiding them in an Agent score.

### Reversible experiment

Choose the smallest action that can change the decision: interview, observation, paper prototype, manual service, landing test, replay, simulation, expert check, or limited pilot. Define what result would support, weaken, or falsify the idea.

### Trade-off declaration

When a contradiction cannot be optimized away, state the chosen priority, accepted downside, safeguard, owner, and revisit trigger. Do not present a human value choice as an objective optimum.

### Parking and rejection

Preserve the reason, evidence, and revisit condition when an idea is parked or rejected. Closure is useful; do not force every discussion toward adoption.

## 5. Question and round design

Choose questions by expected decision impact, not curiosity. Good questions distinguish options, expose a boundary, reveal a stakeholder, or change the next action.

Typical progression:

1. **Meaning:** What change are you really trying to create?
2. **Beneficiary:** Who experiences the problem, and what do they do now?
3. **Principle:** What must remain true even if the form changes?
4. **Trade-off:** Which desirable qualities conflict, and which matters more?
5. **Evidence:** What belief could invalidate the idea?
6. **Commitment:** What is the smallest next step you are willing to take?

Do not ask all six. Ask one to three that unlock the current stage.

End a round when the user has a meaningful choice, correction, or experiment. Summarize what changed since the prior round. If no new decision-relevant insight appears, close or move to evidence rather than generating another meeting.
